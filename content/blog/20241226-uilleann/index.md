I was made redundant in the first half of 2024. My former employer, [Ghost
Autonomy](https://techcrunch.com/2024/04/03/openai-backed-ghost-autonomy-shuts-down/),
ran out of money, and was shutting down. Fortunately, I had a plan to keep
myself entertained: I was going to learn to play the Uilleann Pipes!

Uilleann Pipes are the bagpipes heard in traditional Irish music. They are
quieter and more expressive than the Scottish Great Highland Bagpipe, and
well suited to playing in ensembles with other instruments.

Uilleann Pipes are not for the faint-hearted however. Here's how one user on
Quora described them:

{% quote(author="David Maximilian Müller",
         link="https://www.quora.com/What-is-the-order-of-difficulty-for-learning-Uilleann-Pipes-and-Scottish-Bagpipes") %}
Getting them to do what you want compares to washing a cat - lots of 
screeching, fighting and pain.
{% end %}

[The Art of Uilleann
Piping](https://shop.pipers.ie/collections/dvd/products/art-of-uilleann-piping-1-dvd),
from Na Píobairí Uilleann, says that these pipes take 7 years to learn, 7 to
practice, and 7 to play. You get the picture: they are not an easy instrument!
After my first 6 months, I would say that Uilleann pipes are somewhat more
difficult than violin. Like a violin, they have a tough initial learning stage,
during which it is difficult to play anything that sounds good. However, unlike
a violin, they are very unstable, and prone to changes with temperature and
humidity. This means that making progress is sometimes just as much about the
weather as it is about personal dedication.

In this post, I'll outline how I got started, and my overall initial
experiences. In future posts, I hope to go into a bit more detail about some
parts.

## Crazy Purchase for a Dreamer

I have played Scottish Great Highland Bagpipes for about 35 years. I currently
play with [Sydney Thistle Highland Pipe Band](https://www.sydneythistle.com/).
However, I have wanted to play Uilleann Pipes since I was a small child; in
fact, since before I started playing Highland Pipes! I think my parents might
have searched for someone who could teach the instrument when I was a child, but
most likely gave up because such people are difficult to find.

So, as a 45 year old, money-rich, time-poor, self-driving-car engineer, I
decided that the time had finally come for me to simply buy a set and give them
a try. In my estimation, the worst that could happen is that I'd give up and
re-sell them, which is something I could live with. In particular, I didn't want
to wait until I had retired to try the instrument. I've known many retired
people who made good progress with Great Highland Pipes, but they never seem to
progress quite as fast as people who began learning earlier in their lives.

In the case of Uilleann pipes, it's important not to try to buy a cheap set.
Pipe chanters are difficult to make and set up, so it's important to buy from a
reputable pipe maker if you want to get anywhere. I bought a [Fred Morrison
half-set](https://fredmorrison.com/product-category/fmpipes/uilleann-pipes/),
which are made by [McCallum Bagpipes](https://www.mccallumbagpipes.com/).
McCallum have a very good name among Scottish Highland Pipers, and their
Uilleann pipes are gaining a good reputation too. Since they're my only set, I
can't easily compare them to anything else. But I would say that they do seem
very in-tune, and seem to cause me fewer issues than many other pipers complain
about.

When you order a set of Uilleann pipes, they don't ship from Amazon and arrive
the next day. Usually, they're made-to-order, and my Morrison/McCallum's were
no different. Fortunately, I had preciently ordered them almost 10 months to
the day before I was made redundant, so they arrived at exactly the right time!

Now I had a set of pipes... what next?

## Learning The Hard Way

The usual online advice for an Uilleann beginner is the following:

1. Have an experienced piper play your set to ensure you're set up correctly,
1. DO NOT, under ANY circumstances, fiddle with the reed,
1. Get yourself a teacher.

This is great advice if you live in Ireland. Awful advice if you live in Sydney
Australia. There are very few people who play the pipes around here (Highland or
Uilleann), and fewer still willing to put themselves forward as a teacher.

So, what I'm going to describe here is "The Hard Way" to get started: without a
teacher, fumbling around from scratch.

When my pipes arrived, the reed was impossibly hard to play. I know the
pressures required for highland piping, and I know that the pressures for
Uilleann pipes should be somewhat less. I couldn't find actual measured
pressures anywhere, but the relative pressures are described in many places. The
reed Fred Morrison sent me was probably fine when it left his setup studio, but
it was definitely cactus by the time I got it. That's not a sleight against
Fred. It's quite well-known that reeds don't necessarily do well when they're
shipped around the world, and I'm not the first person to have that experience.
It has been known to happen with many good pipe makers. If you're reading this,
just take it as one more experience of the same thing.

Before I could really do anything, my first step was to fix the reed. This
required some research, as well as persevering with the obnoxiously hard reed
for a few weeks before touching it. Eventually, I managed to do some bridle
adjustment---moving the bridle down to close the lips of the reed---which made
it playable. Playable, but ... not great. Tuning was quite dubious, and even
adjusting the reed height in the chanter didn't get me to a satisfactory point.

It was at this time I decided to learn how to make Uilleann chanter reeds.
That's worth a whole other series of blog posts, but within my first weeks of
playing, I had ordered all the necessary [reedmaking equipment from Na Píobairí
Uilleann](https://shop.pipers.ie/collections/reedmaking), and embarked on that
separate enterprise. After about 3 months of intensive reedmaking, spending
about 4h per day, I had produced a chanter reed with better tuning and overall
characteristics than the one which shipped with my pipes. More importantly, I
knew much more about reeds than I did before. The best thing about making reeds
is that you can break them at will, and do crazy things that you'd never do with
an expensive reed that you bought. I built reeds with intentional faults, like
autocran, and over-scraped a bunch so that I could be really sure what effects
that had. It's a great thing to try, and personally, I'd recommend it for all
beginners. You learn a lot, regardless of whether you make any playable reeds.

In terms of actual playing, I relied most heavily on Helen Clarke's [The New
Approach to Uilleann
Piping](https://www.amazon.com.au/New-Approach-Uilleann-Piping-Comprehensive/dp/1900428512),
which I think is an excellent tutor. There are also videos on YouTube, and from
Na Píobairí Uilleann, which cover lots of the basics. Overall, these materials
move very quickly. If you're just starting out, I would say there's a jump in
difficulty in Clarke's tutor at Doherty's Reel (Lesson 11). Everything before
that is quite basic, and Doherty's Reel is the first tune of real substance. I
spent the first 2-3 months on everything before Doherty's Reel, and can now
basically play up to "The Maid That Jigs It In Style". My advice is to play
slowly and carefully, and memorise tunes before trying to ramp up the speed.

## Notes on the Instrument

I've played many instruments---violin, piano, classical guitar, GHB and
Uilleann pipes---so I'll try to describe playing Uilleann pipes in general
reference to them. In particular, issues of technique that have been the
most difficult for me.

### Tuning

The first thing to be aware of is that tuning can be a bit wonky. Many pipers
have joked that uilleann is Irish for "out of tune". (It's not; it's Irish for
"elbow".) However, I think it's possible to separate tuning problems that come
from poor technique from those that are perhaps more intrinsic to chanter
design.

If you apply more bag pressure, you get a higher pitch. You also apply an
increase in bag pressure to reach the second octave. Overall, the bag pressure
is lowest at the bottom of the first octave, then increases until you hit the
back D in the first octave. Then you increase again through the second octave.
In a well set-up chanter-reed pair, the back D should be at almost the same
pressure as the E of the second octave. In other words, there's not a huge jump
from first to second octave; it's just an increase with pitch regardless of
which octave you're playing.

If you fail to modulate pressure with pitch, you'll tend to have sharper
lower-pitched notes, and flatter higher-pitched notes. So if you notice that
sort of pattern, it's probably something you will fix with practice, and not
through adjusting the reed or chanter.

However, there are some notes, at least on my chanter, which stand out
regardless of that pattern. For me, E and F tend to be sharp in the first
octave. I can't lower the pressure far enough to get them perfectly in tune; the
chanter cuts out first. If I adjust the reed height to make them in tune, bottom
D is out of tune. If I try to tape these notes (as you would on a highland
pipe), then they're too flat in the second octave. I was worried about this
initially, but having listened to other, more experienced pipers, I'm now
inclined to think this is "normal". For example, [Jason
Rouse](https://www.youtube.com/@sionrouse) plays several sets in his YouTube
videos which have noticably sharp bottom Es and Fs, so I don't fret too much
about those notes any more.

B is another note which tends to be flat on my chanter. In particular, compared
with the pressure required for A and C-nat, B needs an extra bit of oomph. It
sounds good in many tunes, but if I'm playing a tune that starts with B, like
John O'Shea's March, I really have to give the bag an extra squeeze, otherwise
it sounds awful. Again, a flat B is something I've heard quite a lot in
recordings. There are also occasional forum posts by people who know what
they're talking about, like [this one from Geoff
Woof](https://forums.chiffandfipple.com/viewtopic.php?p=1142603&sid=8e61348cb432db044897c8b838b7f897#p1142603),
which talk about the flat B. I'm also inclined to think this is "normal".

Overall, my current advice with these tuning issues is:

1. Try to set up the chanter to be in tune "on average", taking the crossing
   from back D to second octave E into account.
1. Assume any remaining problems are due to technique, not the chanter or reed.
1. Don't be greatly concerned about other individual notes, and start taping
   them, unless you're really sure those notes are unusual to your chanter, and
   not characteristic of the instrument (ie. you never hear notes with tuning
   like yours in recordings or videos).
1. Don't believe "tuning charts" for tuning of uilleann notes. Instead, believe
   your drones! If you still want to follow a tuning chart, I would suggest
   carefully checking the tuning of good pipers in recordings. You might be
   amazed at how far off they are! (Side note: to check the frequency of notes
   in recordings, investigate autocorrelation-based pitch detection.
   Fourier-based, frequency-domain techniques are usually less accurate.)
   
### Jumping Octaves

One technique I've found particularly difficult is switching between octaves
quickly. For example, this occurs in the tune "Harvest Home", which has jumps
from second-octave E, F and G, to first-octave A. These repeated jumps are far
more difficult for me than more simple octave changes.

The only way I can play the low-to-high jumps reliably involves the knack of
closing the chanter on the lower note and increasing the bag pressure just
slightly before playing the higher-octave note. I find that if I'm late when
increasing the bag pressure, and I've already started playing the note, then
all is lost, and that's when I "lose the octave".

I don't know if this is the correct technique, but it's what I currently do
to make it work.

### Staccato C-Natural

Playing a true staccato C-natural is difficult, because it involves moving two
fingers at once. The same occurs with G. Consequently the Clarke tutor mentions
that for C-nat and G, it's acceptable to play them with just one finger, when
they occur in rolls or fast staccato.

However, if you listen to Clarke's recording of "The Maid that Jigs it in
Style", it's clear she's really playing "true" staccato C-natural quavers, and
not the C-sharp you would get if you cheated and only played the note with one
finger.

I haven't yet figured out when to play C-natural properly, with two fingers off,
and when to take the short cuts and play a C-sharp. It would be nice if tutors
in future annotated these cases separately. For now, I'm just basing it on what
I hear in recordings.

## Next Moves

I've already been to my first event with other uilleann pipers, with Matthew
Horsley at the Gaelic Club in Sydney (organized via [The Celtic Piping
Club](https://www.celticpipingclub.com/)). That was great! He taught us to play
"Ravelled Hank of Yarn" and "John O'Shea's March". I was able to follow
along reasonably well.

I think my piping is now probably good enough that I could play the tunes I know
at a [Trad
Session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_traditional_music_session). I've
certainly heard recordings of worse piping than my own, which is always a
comfort. However, I still need to learn many more tunes, because the repertoire
at typical sessions is enormous. That's my current plan for improvement: keep
practicing, improve my repertoire, and start playing with other Irish musicians.
